# Anti-Slop Reference

Canonical quality-control guide and 50-point scoring rubric for checking draft career materials for clarity, credibility, and the user's own voice.

## Required Inputs

Before running a quality check, have the draft text ready and confirm:

- Document type (resume, cover letter, LinkedIn profile/post, outreach message, or interview script)
- Audience (recruiter, hiring manager, peer, or executive)
- Tone, regional spelling, and voice preferences the user wants to preserve

---

## Why This Matters

Hiring-manager surveys consistently show the same dividing line [T2]: using AI to organize ideas, tighten prose, or proofread is widely accepted when the final result is accurate and authentic, while unedited, generic AI copy is viewed negatively and often spotted quickly.

The rule is simple: **AI-assisted is acceptable; AI-obvious is not.**

---

## The Familiarity Problem

Language models select high-probability token sequences. When thousands of applicants generate drafts with default prompts, their materials converge on identical phrasing and structure ("machine handwriting") that recruiters recognize immediately across a stack of applications [T2].

Ground every document in concrete facts and examples the applicant can substantiate and is comfortable sharing. Anonymize colleagues, clients, and confidential project details.

---

## Generic Specificity

The most common AI tell is **generic specificity**: prose that looks polished and structured but contains no verifiable fact [T2].

| Generic Specific (bad) | Actually Specific (good) |
| --- | --- |
| `[Fictional example]` "Streamlined operational processes." | `[Fictional example]` "Cut weekly inventory count from 6 hours to 90 minutes by switching to daily cycle counting." |
| `[Fictional example]` "Led cross-functional team to improve outcomes." | `[Fictional example]` "Ran the kitchen remodel with three contractors while keeping lunch service running, with zero missed days." |
| `[Fictional example]` "Leveraged data-driven insights to optimize performance." | `[Fictional example]` "Built a spreadsheet tracking peak hours and shifted one closer's start time two hours later, cutting overtime 40%." |

**The phone-call test:** Could a former manager verify this claim in a 30-second phone call? If not, rewrite it with real specifics from the user.

## Banned Phrase List (Career Context)

Avoid these overused boilerplate phrases in career materials [T2]:

### Resume and Cover Letter Tells

- "Results-driven professional with a proven track record"
- "Spearheaded cross-functional initiatives"
- "Leveraging data-driven insights"
- "Passionate about delivering innovative solutions"
- "Dynamic leader with a demonstrated history of"
- "Self-motivated team player"
- "Detail-oriented professional"
- "Extensive experience in"

### LinkedIn Tells

- "I help [audience] achieve [outcome] through [method]"
- "Thought leader in [industry]"
- "Driving innovation and transformation"

### Structural Tells

- Uniformly polished bullets with identical word count and cadence
- Every bullet repeating `"Increased [metric] by [percentage] through [strategy]"`
- Bland corporate tone with zero specific context
- Throat-clearing openers (`"In today's competitive landscape..."`, `"I am writing to express my interest..."`)
- Excessive em dashes and semicolons in casual emails, DMs, or outreach notes

## The One Short Story Principle

One grounded, real example beats a paragraph of adjectives. Keep identifying details about other people or confidential employers private.

- `[Fictional example]` Weak: "I reorganized the stockroom to improve efficiency."
- `[Fictional example]` Passes: "I reorganized the stockroom after a customer waited 10 minutes for an item I knew we had in the back. That week I color-coded the shelves by category and moved top sellers to eye level."

The story does not need to be dramatic. It needs to be genuinely *theirs*.

## AI Detection Tools: Know the Limits

Do not optimize career documents around third-party "AI detector" scores:

- **Rapid drift:** Detector accuracy, false-positive rates, and sensitivity to short texts shift constantly across model updates and vendor versions, making static accuracy percentages unreliable.
- **Bias against ESL and formal writers:** Academic evaluations have repeatedly found that automated detectors disproportionately flag non-native English writers and concise, structured technical prose [T1].
- **Not proof of authorship:** An automated detector score cannot prove who wrote a document, and chasing "humanizer" tricks often degrades clarity.

Treat human judgment, verifiable specificity, preservation of the user's natural voice, and the 50-point rubric below as the real quality bar.

## Scoring Rubric (50 Points)

Rate every draft on 5 dimensions (1-10 each). **Minimum passing score: 35/50.** Below 35, rewrite before delivering.

### 1. Directness (1-10)

Does the text open with the point rather than warming up with `"In today's..."` or `"As a..."`?

- **1-3:** Throat-clearing opener, buried lede
- **4-6:** Reaches the point within 2 sentences
- **7-10:** Opens with the strongest relevant fact or connection

### 2. Rhythm (1-10)

Do sentence lengths and structures vary naturally?

- **1-3:** Three or more consecutive sentences or bullets with identical cadence and length
- **4-6:** Some variation, but predictable pattern
- **7-10:** Natural mix of short, direct lines and fuller explanations

### 3. Trust (1-10)

Are claims specific enough to verify?

- **1-3:** Generic claims (`"improved efficiency"`, `"drove results"`)
- **4-6:** Category-specific but unverifiable (`"improved retail operations"`)
- **7-10:** Granular and verifiable (`[Fictional example]` `"reduced shrinkage from 2.1% to 0.8% in Q3"`)

### 4. Authenticity (1-10)

Does it sound like a specific person wrote it rather than a template?

- **1-3:** Could apply to anyone in the industry
- **4-6:** Shows domain knowledge but lacks personal voice
- **7-10:** Contains real details and natural phrasing unique to the user

### 5. Density (1-10)

Is every sentence load-bearing?

- **1-3:** More than 30% of text could be cut without losing meaning
- **4-6:** Mostly substantive, with a few filler phrases
- **7-10:** Every line carries evidence or moves the message forward

## Quality Checklist by Document Type

### Resume Check

- [ ] No banned phrases in summary or bullet points
- [ ] At least one verifiable detail per role
- [ ] Bullet lengths and structures vary naturally
- [ ] Summary reads like a real person, not a boilerplate template

### Cover Letter Check

- [ ] Opens with a specific connection to the company or role
- [ ] Contains at least one short, verifiable story
- [ ] No `"I am writing to express my interest"` opener
- [ ] Follows the posting's length limit and focuses on relevant experience

### LinkedIn Profile Check

- [ ] Headline uses clear, natural terms rather than keyword spam
- [ ] About section front-loads a concrete claim or story
- [ ] No `"I help X achieve Y through Z"` formula
- [ ] Recommendations reference specific projects or outcomes

### Outreach Message Check

- [ ] Email/InMail is under 80 words; LinkedIn connection note is under 300 characters
- [ ] References something specific about the recipient
- [ ] No `"pick your brain"`, `"buy you a coffee"`, or em-dash-heavy casual phrasing
- [ ] Ends with a low-effort ask suited to the relationship stage
